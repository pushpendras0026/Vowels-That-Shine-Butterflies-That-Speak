DA321 · one equation, two worlds

A vowel has a colour.
A butterfly has a voice.

When you say “ah”, your vocal tract behaves like a chain of short tubes of different widths. A Morpho butterfly’s wing scale is a stack of very thin layers of different materials. Sound bouncing between tube sections and light bouncing between layers follow the same equation, so we can translate one into the other exactly: every vowel becomes a coloured film, and every film can be made to speak.

Each vowel is a pair of discs: R = colour reflected by the vowel’s film (how a wing is seen), T = colour of light transmitted through it. Both mappings are exact; they differ only in the common layer thickness (full-band 199.86 nm puts the 0–7.5 kHz speech band once across the visible; brief 428.27 nm wraps one period, so vowels crowd into violet). The bridge in one line

r = Ai − Ai+1Ai + Ai+1  ⟷  r = ni − ni+1ni + ni+1

Tube cross-section area A plays the role of refractive index n. If every tube section takes the sound one sample to cross and back, and every layer has the same optical thickness (n·d = 428.27 nm), the two systems share one polynomial — so the film’s transmission spectrum is the vowel’s filter, wrapped onto 400–750 THz.

film→layer peeling→all-pole filter→glottal buzz→voice

What is exact, and what is not

exact The tube ⇄ film correspondence: reflection coefficients, the shared polynomial, power transfer = transmittance, LPC ⇄ layer peeling, and the frequency mapping. Lossless, non-dispersive layers, normal incidence (angle effects are computed exactly with the transfer-matrix method). Checked numerically to ~1e‑12 for all 36 vowels.

approximate A real glottis is not a closed end: we use an illustrative termination (reflection −0.9). “Realizable” films squeeze the needed refractive indices (up to ~650 in ideal mode!) into real materials (1.0–2.5), which blurs the formants. Butterfly stacks are parametric look-alikes, not measured wings.

artistic Display brightness normalisation, glows, flight and wing-flap animation, the neon room.

Honest finding: vowels are violet. Mapping one period of the tube’s response onto 400–750 THz puts the speech band that carries vowel identity (0–2.3 kHz) at 650–750 THz — the deep violet edge of vision, where our eyes are least sensitive. So all vowels come out violet/lavender: their hue spans only ≈307°–322°, and neighbouring vowels differ by a median ΔE₀₀ of about 4–8 (just noticeable is ≈1–2; some pairs are ≈0.1, i.e. indistinguishable). Ideal-mode films also transmit very little light (Y ≈ 0.05–0.6 %), which is why colours are shown brightness-normalised.

A shifted mapping that is also exact was tested and separated vowels worse, so we kept the canonical one and report the limitation instead of hiding it. Details: Results.

That finding is for the brief mapping (layers 428.27 nm). The isomorphism holds for any common layer thickness, so the default full-band mapping (199.86 nm) spreads 0–7.5 kHz once across 400–750 THz and the same vowels become clearly different colours (median ΔE₀₀ ≈ 16 for realizable films, reflected).
